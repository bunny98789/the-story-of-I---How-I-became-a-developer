THE STORY OF I

A story of a kid, a Chromebook, and the long road to becoming a game developer

DISCLAIMER: This is a work of creative non-fiction. The events narrated reflect the author’s best recollections of true events. Some minor details, timelines, and dialogues have been dramatized for narrative flow and entertainment purposes.

CHAPTER ONE — THE BUTTON

In fourth grade, he had no idea what he was looking for.

He was just sitting at school, doing what kids did when they had a little freedom: browsing the Chrome Web Store.

There were games. Extensions. Random tools he would install and forget about.

And then he saw it.

Construct 5.

He didn't know what it was. He didn't know what game development meant. He didn't know what a game engine was. He certainly didn't know that one random click would eventually turn into years of coding, thousands upon thousands of lines of JavaScript, lost projects, rebuilt projects, and a dream that would follow him through middle school and into high school.

He installed it.

He opened it.

He stared at it.

And then he deleted it.

That was it.

At least for the moment.

He had no idea that the story had already begun.

CHAPTER TWO — LUNCH PERIOD

Fifth grade came.

School was going well. Every once in a while, he earned a special reward for being a smart kid. One of those rewards was getting to spend lunch in his math teacher's room, where he could sit inside and play games instead of going outside.

One day, he found Construct 5 again.

Something about it looked familiar.

He installed it. This time, he actually tried.

He made games.

They were tiny. Simple. Probably terrible by the standards of a professional developer.

But he didn't care.

To him, they were incredible.

He had made something. Something that hadn't existed before. He had put something on a screen and made it respond to him.

That feeling was different.

There was something addictive about it. You changed something. You pressed a button. Something happened. You changed something else. Something else happened.

For a fifth grader, that was magic.

Then he started discovering the catch.

Construct 5 wasn't exactly free. A huge amount of what he wanted was locked behind a paywall. He could see the possibilities, but he couldn't reach them.

Eventually, frustration won.

He quit.

Again.

CHAPTER THREE — SCRATCH

Sixth grade.

A friend told him about something called Scratch.

He was skeptical. Why would he want to use some weird block-coding website?

Still, he agreed.

Scratch was different. It didn't demand that he understand everything immediately. He could drag blocks around. He could experiment. He could break things and immediately try again.

His first big project was a clicker game.

There was one problem.

He didn't understand variables very well.

He wanted the player to spend money.

But somehow, every time the player spent money...

they got richer.

The game had accidentally become the world's worst economy.

His smarter friend eventually explained that variables could change by negative numbers. That tiny lesson stuck with him. It was one of hundreds of little moments where something confusing suddenly became obvious.

Scratch became a home.

He spent sixth grade making games. He experimented. He learned. He messed up. He tried again.

And somewhere during that year, a thought began forming.

Not just:

"I like making games."

But:

"I want to be a game developer."

CHAPTER FOUR — ANOTHER ENGINE

Then came GDevelop.

It reminded him of Construct. Except it felt more open. More accessible. More possible.

He made a 3D game using a template. He attempted to make a Pokémon-like game. That one didn't work out.

There were projects that lasted days. Projects that lasted weeks. Projects that died halfway through.

But that didn't matter.

Every project taught him something.

He wasn't trying to make masterpieces yet. He was learning how games worked.

Sixth grade became seventh grade, and he kept going.

Scratch. GDevelop. Game after game.

Some were basic. Some were surprisingly ambitious.

Eventually, he even made a 3D game in Scratch. He experimented with other people's projects. He modified Griffpatch's Paper Minecraft. He kept pushing the boundaries of what he thought he could do.

Then seventh grade took a turn.

Something happened at a sleepover. Things got weird. The details weren't important to the story.

What mattered was what came afterward.

He was hurt.

Deeply.

For a while, the screen felt cold. The screen that had always been a place where he could create became something he didn't want to look at.

But eventually, he came back.

Not because everything was suddenly okay. Just because making things was still part of him.

By the end of seventh grade, there were games again. Projects again. Ideas again.

And then came eighth grade.

CHAPTER FIVE — THE BROWSER

Eighth grade hit hard.

But something interesting happened.

He reinstalled Web Maker.

At first, he made simple games. Nothing enormous. Nothing that seemed destined for greatness.

Then he discovered something that changed the way he thought about browser games.

His Chromebook had a built-in editor. It could run JavaScript. It could use localStorage.

And with some help from AI, suddenly the browser wasn't just a place to play games.

It was a place to build them.

He started making project after project.

He learned HTML. CSS. JavaScript.

He started understanding things that would have looked like nonsense a year earlier.

Then he went back to Scratch for a while. And he made one of the best games he had created.

It worked.

It wasn't just something he enjoyed making. His mom actually enjoyed playing it.

He loved watching his mom try his game. The bugs she found. The way she was happy when she beat it.

Then she asked him something he didn't expect.

"Can you make a mobile version so I can play it on my phone?"

That hit differently.

Someone else wanted to play something he had made.

Not because it was a school assignment. Not because he had asked them to test it.

Because they actually enjoyed it.

The attention felt good.

He wanted more.

And in December, he started his biggest project yet.

He called it:

NO WAY HOME

CHAPTER SIX — NO WAY HOME

At first, No Way Home wasn't supposed to become No Way Home.

It started with something tiny.

A text effect.

He wanted text to fade in. Then fade out.

That was it.

But games have a dangerous habit.

They grow.

First came a player. Then items. Then enemies. Then structures. Then crafting. Then cooking. Then a world. Then procedural generation. Then bosses. Then leveling. Then stats. Then abilities. Then an inventory.

Not the hotbar.

A real inventory system.

One he had built himself.

He kept adding. And adding. And adding.

The project grew from a little experiment into something enormous.

Ten thousand lines.

10,000 lines of code.

For a kid who had once struggled to understand why his clicker game was giving players money instead of taking it away, this was almost unbelievable.

No Way Home became more than a game.

It became proof.

Proof that he could build something big. Something complicated. Something people might actually recognize.

Maybe this would be the game.

The one that made people say:

"Who made this?"

And he'd be able to answer:

"I did."

CHAPTER SEVEN — THE ASSETS

Then came the problem.

The game was using assets that weren't his.

At first, that hadn't seemed like a huge deal. But the more he learned about game development, the more obvious the problem became.

If he published the game using other people's work, he could get into serious trouble.

No Way Home couldn't be released like that.

For a moment, it felt like the entire project had hit a wall.

Then came the solution.

He and his brother would redraw the assets.

All of them.

More than a hundred.

One by one.

They made new versions from scratch. Some assets had already been hand-drawn, which was a relief.

Then they discovered something else.

His brother had taken a frame from a GIF he found online and used it for a major asset.

That hurt.

But they fixed it.

The project survived.

And somewhere in all of that work, he learned another lesson:

"If you're going to put your name on something, you need to own what you put into it."

No Way Home kept growing. It had become massive.

And for the first time, the idea of actually publishing one of his big games didn't feel impossible.

Maybe this really was going to be the game.

CHAPTER EIGHT — GONE

Then eighth grade ended.

He had plans for the summer. He was going to keep coding. Keep improving No Way Home. Keep building.

Then, after a state test, something happened that he couldn't undo.

He opened his files app.

His files were gone.

All of them.

He stared at the screen for a while, hoping something would bring them back.

He checked his file's history.

They were gone.

The project he'd spent so long building had disappeared.

The assets. The code. The systems. The thousands of lines. The inventory. The world.

Everything.

Almost everything.

There was one thing left.

A published version on Discord.

A final snapshot of what No Way Home had been.

But the actual project was gone.

It hurt.

A lot.

He stopped working on it.

For a while, No Way Home just sat there in his mind. Not finished. Not exactly dead. Just... unreachable.

Eventually, in May, he tried again. He worked on it a little over the summer.

But things had changed.

He was learning other things. He moved into Roblox Studio. He started new projects.

No Way Home wasn't gone from his mind.

But it wasn't moving forward either.

CHAPTER NINE — THE REBUILD

Ninth grade arrived.

Maybe No Way Home could come back.

There was still a path.

The game had an enormous game.js file. Around 8,000 lines.

That was a problem.

So he did what seemed like the smart thing.

He broke it apart.

Thirty smaller files. Cleaner organization. More manageable code.

The old game was being rebuilt into something better.

Then he moved it onto his Chromebook.

And it broke.

Not a small bug.

Not a missing semicolon.

The whole thing just...

didn't work.

He tried to figure it out. AI tried to figure it out. Nothing seemed to solve it.

The project that had already survived lost files, copyright problems, and months of work had encountered another wall.

This time, he quit.

For good.

At least, that's what he told himself.

No Way Home had been his greatest project. The game that was supposed to make him known. The game that had grown from a fading text effect into ten thousand lines. The game where he had built an inventory system from scratch.

And now it was broken.

The original project was gone.

The rebuilt version was broken.

The chapter seemed finished.

CHAPTER TEN — RABBITLABS

But developers have a strange problem.

They don't know how to stop making things.

He started another project.

RabbitLabs.

A breeding simulator with combat elements.

Rabbits spawned. They moved. They generated coins. There was a shop. Eggs. Different rabbits. Systems began connecting to each other.

It became another big project.

Not No Way Home.

But still something.

Then he discovered another world.

MMO games.

Multiplayer.

What if instead of making a game where one person explored a world...

he made a game where multiple people existed in the same world?

That thought wouldn't leave.

RabbitLabs was paused.

And a new idea appeared.

CHAPTER ELEVEN — FROKO.IO

It started as another browser game.

HTML. CSS. JavaScript.

Then came the multiplayer part.

That meant something new.

A server. Node.js. Socket.IO. Rooms. Players. Synchronization.

The project eventually became:

FroKo.io

A real-time top-down multiplayer shooter.

Players could create rooms. Join rooms. Become hosts. See each other's usernames. Move around. Aim. Shoot. Take damage. Die. Respawn. Fight across rounds.

There were obstacles. Bullets. Health. Ammo. Spectators. Game states. A lobby. A server. A real public deployment.

It was hosted on Railway. The frontend lived on GitHub Pages.

This wasn't the kid who had accidentally created an infinite-money clicker anymore.

This was a developer who had spent years learning how games actually worked.

And, naturally...

it broke.

CHAPTER TWELVE — THE FREEZE

One problem was especially annoying.

The game looked fine.

The FPS was around 60.

Ping was around 15–25 milliseconds.

The server was receiving updates.

Everything seemed healthy.

Yet the game felt terrible.

Players would move.

Then suddenly...

freeze.

For about a second.

Then continue.

Again.

And again.

The server was blamed. The network was blamed. The update rate was changed.

16 milliseconds.

25 milliseconds.

Back to 16.

Everything was adjusted.

Nothing made sense.

Then came the logs.

The player updates were arriving constantly.

The server was doing its job.

So what was wrong?

Eventually, the answer appeared.

The rendering function.

drawGame().

It had been missing. The render loop was calling something that wasn't properly there.

One fix.

One function.

One moment.

And suddenly—

the freezing was gone.

The game worked.

Not perfectly. Not finished.

But it worked.

He made a backup. A release. A stable version.

FroKo.io v0.7.0 — Stable.

For once, he wasn't staring at a broken project.

He was staring at a playable game.

And tomorrow, someone special was going to play it.

His mom.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN — THE COINS

The game wasn't finished yet.

There were still systems to build. Combat needed more depth. There were weapons to design. Cosmetics. Stats. A scoreboard. Spectating improvements. A proper death screen. Sudden death. Better rounds. A real shop. Accounts. Maybe someday.

But even while the code was frozen for playtesting, there was something he could do.

He could make the assets.

So he opened Canva.

The first currency was obvious.

FroKoins.

Gold coins. Chunky. Cartoony. Simple.

The letters:

FK

printed onto the face.

Then there was the rarer currency.

KoKash.

Purple. A little more special. A little cleaner. But still meant to belong in the same goofy game.

Then came weapons.

And cosmetics.

Funny cosmetics.

Ideas that didn't need to make sense.

They just needed to make someone laugh.

The game was beginning to develop its own identity.

It wasn't No Way Home.

It didn't have to be.

It was something new.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN — THE DEADLINE

September 6.

Late at night.

The code was frozen. The assets were being made. Tomorrow was the playtest.

And there was now a deadline.

September 30.

By midnight.

FroKo.io would reach:

v1.0.0

There was still a lot to do.

But this time, he knew something he hadn't known when he started No Way Home.

A game didn't have to become enormous before it could be finished.

He didn't need fifty weapons. He didn't need a hundred cosmetics. He didn't need every idea he had ever imagined.

He needed a game that worked.

A game that people could play.

A game that felt complete.

And then he could build more.

Version 1.1.

1.2.

2.0.

Whatever came next.

There was one joke that kept coming back, though.

A ridiculous idea.

A creature that absolutely did not need to exist.

The Floofy Bunny.

Maybe someday.

CHAPTER FIFTEEN — WHAT WAS LOST

There was still one thing he couldn't completely let go of.

No Way Home.

He wanted to revive it.

He knew the chapter had ended.

But that didn't mean he wanted the story to disappear.

The worst part wasn't losing ten thousand lines of code. It wasn't losing the assets. It wasn't even losing the inventory system.

It was losing the feeling of knowing:

"I built this."

That inventory system had been one of the moments when he realized how far he'd come.

He had built something complicated.

Something that worked.

Something that had once seemed impossible.

And now the most important parts were gone.

Poof.

There was no magical button that could bring the exact code back.

Maybe it could never be reconstructed exactly.

Maybe No Way Home, as it once existed, really was gone.

That hurt.

Because he hadn't just wanted it to be a game.

He had wanted it to be the game.

The one that made him known.

The one that proved he was a great developer.

The one people would remember.

For a moment, the loss felt ridiculous.

It was just a game.

Just code.

Just files.

So why did it hurt this much?

It wasn't just a game.

It was years.

It was fourth grade. Fifth grade. Sixth grade. Seventh grade. Eighth grade.

It was the first time he opened an engine. The first broken clicker. The first variable. The first 3D experiment. The first huge project. The first time his mom genuinely enjoyed something he made. The first time he realized he could build systems instead of just games.

No Way Home had been there through all of it.

Losing it meant losing a piece of that journey.

But there was something the lost files couldn't take.

He still remembered how to build.

CHAPTER SIXTEEN — THE NEXT GAME

The story wasn't ending.

That was the strange thing.

The project that was supposed to make him a great developer had disappeared.

And yet here he was.

Still coding. Still designing. Still making assets. Still thinking about weapons. Still imagining ridiculous cosmetics. Still planning currencies. Still debugging multiplayer servers late at night. Still trying to make something that other people could play.

He had started with a random Chrome Web Store discovery.

He had quit Construct. Returned to it. Quit again.

Found Scratch.

Found GDevelop.

Made games that only he thought were good.

Then games other people enjoyed.

He made a massive game.

Lost it.

Tried to rebuild it.

Lost that battle too.

Made another game.

Paused it.

Started something new.

And somehow, after all of that...

He was still here.

The projects changed. The tools changed. The code changed.

But the reason never really did.

He liked making things.

He liked seeing an idea become real.

He liked pressing a button and watching something happen because he made it happen.

No Way Home might never return in its original form.

Maybe one day a successor would rise from its ashes.

Maybe it wouldn't.

Maybe the original inventory system would remain forever lost.

But it had already done something important.

It had taught him that he could build something enormous.

And now FroKo.io had a different job.

It didn't have to replace No Way Home.

It didn't have to erase it.

It just had to move the story forward.

EPILOGUE — 11:00 PM

September 6, 2026.

The clock was approaching eleven.

The assets were ready.

FroKoins were gold.

KoKash was purple.

The code was stable.

Tomorrow, his mom would play the game.

And somewhere in a Discord server, an old published build of No Way Home still existed.

A frozen piece of another time.

A reminder of the game that once seemed like it would define everything.

He could miss it. He could be proud of it. He could wish he'd never lost it.

All at once.

But tomorrow was coming.

And tomorrow, someone would connect to a server.

A room would be created.

A player would appear on a battlefield.

And somewhere on a Chromebook screen, a game he'd built would begin.

Not the game he thought would make him a great developer.

Something else.

Something newer.

Something that existed because of everything that came before it.

The story hadn't ended with No Way Home.

It had simply changed names.

The screen went dark.

Then, somewhere in the code, a server waited for its first connection.

FroKo.io.

v0.7.0 — Stable.

Next stop: v1.0.0.

And after that?

Who knew.

Maybe weapons.

Maybe cosmetics.

Maybe accounts.

Maybe a shop.

Maybe a hundred new bugs.

Maybe even...

the Floofy Bunny.

The cursor blinked.

And the developer kept going.
